The new Congress can block a president. Governing will be harder.

Democratic majorities gain oversight and spending leverage, but the veto, Senate procedure and the calendar will shape what becomes law.

Proposed edition: November 5, 2026 · Prepared 2026-11-05T07:01:11Z · By The State Desk

Democrats emerged from the November 3 midterms with control of both chambers of Congress, according to reporting by Reuters and the Associated Press on November 4. That is a consequential transfer of legislative power. It is not, by itself, a transfer of executive power: President Donald Trump remains in office, with the constitutional veto and executive authorities intact.

The most immediate change may be institutional rather than legislative. The House can convene oversight hearings and authorize investigations; the Senate can scrutinize nominees and hold committee inquiries. Both chambers will have leverage over appropriations and the terms of any debt-limit deal. Reuters reported on November 4 that the incoming majorities were already considering how to use those powers, including in debates over the administration’s foreign policy.

The legislative arithmetic remains more difficult. Article I, Section 7 of the Constitution requires two-thirds votes in each chamber to override a presidential veto. The Senate’s standing rules generally require three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn to invoke cloture on ordinary legislation. That means a simple Senate majority is not necessarily enough to bring a contested bill to a final vote. Exceptions and procedural variations matter, but none should be mistaken for a general exemption from those constraints.

Timing matters, too. Election winners do not automatically acquire congressional authority on election night. The Twentieth Amendment sets the start of congressional terms at noon on January 3, unless Congress changes the date by law. Until then, the sitting Congress remains responsible for appropriations and other pending business.

The next practical tests are therefore likely to concern funding deadlines, oversight and nominations rather than a sudden enactment of an entire campaign platform. Reuters reported ahead of the election that the White House had been preparing options for executive action on tariffs, immigration, housing and spending in anticipation of divided government. Whether those options survive judicial review or legislative scrutiny is a separate question.

The distinction between blocking and building is crucial. A new majority may refuse a proposal, condition appropriations or investigate agency conduct without securing the president’s signature on an alternative. That could produce durable oversight and some negotiated concessions; it could also produce stalemate. The public will have to judge the new Congress not only by its announced priorities, but by what it actually negotiates, funds and enacts.
