CONGRESS / TECHNOLOGY

The next Congress is preparing an AI agenda. Its first test may be electricity bills

Lawmakers are discussing model oversight and consumer liability, but data-center costs offer a more immediate pocketbook argument.

THE STATE DESK · Published Nov. 6, 2026, 1:58 p.m. EST · Updated Nov. 6, 2026, 1:58 p.m. EST

The next Congress is preparing to confront artificial intelligence from several directions: consumer protection, liability, independent testing and the electricity costs of the infrastructure behind it. The proposals are still proposals. Neither the new congressional majority nor a committee hearing can turn them into law on its own.

Reuters reported on November 6 that Democratic lawmakers intend to introduce legislation on data-center power costs, advanced-model safety reviews and the responsibility of AI developers when products cause harm. The emerging agenda would also increase scrutiny of large technology companies through hearings and document requests.

Electricity bills could provide the clearest point of contact with ordinary households. A data center's power demand is an infrastructure issue involving utilities, transmission capacity and state regulators as much as a technology-policy question. Congress can seek testimony and set federal rules, but state utility commissions and existing contracts will continue to shape how costs reach customers.

There is also an important difference between regulating present-day systems and attempting to halt future advanced models. The Associated Press previously reported on a proposal from Sen. Bernie Sanders and Rep. Greg Casar that would establish a new federal AI department and restrict development of certain advanced systems. That earlier bill is not the same thing as the incoming majority's reported legislative plans, and it should not be presented as a settled caucus program.

The White House remains an obstacle to sweeping legislation. A presidential veto would require a two-thirds vote in both chambers to override. Hearings and oversight, by contrast, can proceed through congressional committee authority. The practical test is whether legislators can turn a broad technology debate into enforceable protections without promising powers Congress does not possess.
