Newsom seeks to replace 548 death sentences. The courts still have a say.

The governor's clemency decision is sweeping, but 382 cases require judicial approval and capital punishment remains on California's books.

Prepared November 5, 2026, 8:04 p.m. EST · Last updated November 5, 2026, 8:04 p.m. EST · Attributed news analysis

California Gov. Gavin Newsom announced Thursday that he would commute all 548 death sentences in the state to life imprisonment without the possibility of parole. The announcement marks an extraordinary use of executive clemency, but the legal outcome is not complete: the California Supreme Court must approve commutations involving people convicted of multiple felonies. The Associated Press puts that group at 382 cases.

That distinction is central to what happens next. Subtracting the 382 cases requiring judicial review from the 548 announced commutations leaves 166 cases outside that specific approval category. The calculation does not mean all 166 have already been administratively finalized, and it does not forecast how the court will rule. The Los Angeles Times similarly reports that roughly 40% can be handled directly, with the remainder needing the high court's consent.

Newsom halted executions by moratorium in 2019; California's last execution occurred in 2006, according to Reuters. A moratorium pauses executions without eliminating the statutory death penalty. The commutations, where effective, replace individual death sentences; they do not repeal California's capital punishment law or prevent future prosecutors from seeking death sentences under existing law.

The decision reopens a long-running conflict between the governor's clemency authority and the role of juries, courts and voters. CalMatters reports objections from victims' advocates, who argue that commutations displace punishments imposed after trials and appeals. Newsom has defended his opposition to executions as a moral judgment about state power. Those are distinct positions, and neither resolves the separate legal question now before the court.

For families affected by capital crimes, the immediate practical issue is not whether California has formally abolished the death penalty—it has not—but which sentences become final life-without-parole terms and when. A full accounting will require tracking the Supreme Court's review rather than treating the governor's announcement as the final disposition of every case.
